# The AI-tell detector

The list of constructions that make writing read as machine-written. Check a draft
against every one, then fix it. The fix is almost always the same shape: make a
positive claim, in plain words, and stop when the point lands.

Ordered roughly by how badly each one gives the game away.

---

## 1. Antithesis: "X, not Y" (the big one)

AI defines things by what they aren't. It sounds deep and says little. Both forms are
the same tell:

- Trailing: "Real systems in production, not demos."
- Leading cleft: "It isn't about getting it to run, it's about getting it reliable."

Just say the positive half.

- ❌ "Real systems in production, not demos." → ✅ "They run in production."
- ❌ "You see progress, not promises." → ✅ "You see it working as it's built."
- ❌ "Not just faster, but smarter." → ✅ pick the one true claim and say it.
- ❌ "The part I spend time on isn't getting it to run, it's getting it reliable." → ✅ "The part I spend time on is getting it reliable."

Also watch: "instead of", "rather than", "it's less about X and more about Y".

## 2. Em-dashes for drama

- ❌ "It works — every time." → ✅ "It works every time." (period, comma, or restructure)
- Never use — or – as sentence punctuation. Hyphens in compound words (back-office) are fine.

## 3. The manufactured-profound kicker

A small aphorism bolted onto the end to sound deep. Reads as writerly, not spoken.

- ❌ "…and that's where the real work hides."
- ❌ "…and that's the part nobody talks about."
- ❌ "…and that changes everything."
- ❌ "…and that's the whole game."

Real people just stop, or say it plain ("that's the hard bit"). Cut the flourish.

## 4. The tidy resolution / both-sides bow

Posing a rhetorical question, then answering it with a balanced lesson. It manufactures
closure and usually restates a point already made.

- ❌ "So is it worth it? Maybe. But really it's about whether you trust the output."

Delete the question-and-answer. End on something concrete, a dry aside, or leave the
tension open.

## 5. Rule of three

AI loves triples: "fast, simple, and reliable."

- Use one strong word, two, or an uneven list. Real lists are lumpy.

## 6. GPT-ese vocabulary (ban list)

leverage, robust, seamless, elevate, unlock, empower, streamline, delve, tailored,
cutting-edge, "in today's fast-paced world", "I'm thrilled to", "game-changer",
"supercharge", "navigate the landscape", "at the end of the day".

- Use plain verbs: build, run, cut, find, fix, save, ship.

## 7. Hedged universal audience

- ❌ "Whether you're a solo founder or a large team…" → ✅ name one real reader.

## 8. Over-symmetry

Matching "Pros / Cons", parallel arrow lists, every sentence the same length. Vary it.
Real writing is uneven.

## 9. Summary / echo ending

Restating the paragraph in the last line. Just stop when the point lands.

## 10. The buildup pivot

"But here's the thing…", "Here's the kicker…", "And that's when it hit me…". Don't build
up to a reveal. State it.

---

## Language-specific tells

Most of the above are language-agnostic. Each language has its own fingerprints. Record
the user's in their voice profile. Worked example for Norwegian (the author's own list):

- **Oxford-komma**: no comma before "og" in a list. "raskt, enkelt og trygt", never
  "…enkelt, og trygt". The comma is a translation-from-English fingerprint.
- **Norwenglish / calques**: "på slutten av dagen", "når det er sagt", "ta det til neste
  nivå". Say it in plain Norwegian.
- **Anthropomorphizing**: "AI-en forstår / tenker / vil". Describe what the system does,
  not what it "wants".
- **Filler**: "faktisk", "egentlig", "rett og slett". Cut.

---

## Quick check before shipping

1. Search the text for: ` not `, ` — `, `instead of`, `rather than`, and the ban-list words.
2. Read it out loud. If a clause is only there to set up a contrast, delete it.
3. Does the last line tie a bow? Cut it.
4. Would the person it's supposedly from actually say it this way?
